"I think Maya's burning out."

It's one of the most common things HR leads and founders say to me about their best people. Some are unsure how to respond or support their employee as they struggle to see the difference between a rough patch and burnout. These two can look similar but require very different responses.

Tell someone having a hard week that they're burnt out, and you risk pathologising something a decent weekend would fix. Tell someone who's actually burnt out that they just need to push through and you risk losing them entirely.

Here's how to tell the difference, and what to do once you know.

---

## So, what is burnout?

The World Health Organization doesn't classify burnout as a medical condition. It classifies it as an occupational phenomenon, specifically resulting from chronic workplace stress that hasn't been successfully managed.<sup>1</sup>

That word "chronic" is what needs to be emphasised. Burnout isn't a bad Tuesday. It's what happens when the bad Tuesdays stack up for months without relief.

The WHO names three specific markers:<sup>1</sup>

* Exhaustion that doesn't lift with rest. Not "I need a nap," but a depletion that a weekend, or even a week off, doesn't touch.
* Growing distance or cynicism about the work. Someone who used to care visibly starts checking out, going quiet in meetings, or getting sharper-edged about things that used to just be part of the job.
* A drop in their own sense of effectiveness. They start doubting whether what they do matters, even when their output hasn't actually changed.

A bad week can produce a flicker of all three. Burnout is when all three have settled in and stayed.

---

## Why this distinction matters for you as a manager

Gallup's research on burnout found that 76% of employees experience it at least sometimes, and 28% experience it very often or always.<sup>2</sup>

The same research points to five factors that predict it most reliably: unfair treatment, an unmanageable workload, unclear expectations from managers, lack of manager support, and unreasonable time pressure.<sup>2</sup>

Notice that none of those are about individual resilience. They're about the environment someone is working in.

Burnout isn't primarily a personal failing to be coached out of someone. It's usually a sign that something structural needs attention: workload, clarity, support, or fairness. Treating it as a personal problem when it's a systemic one tends to make people feel worse.

---

## Practical signs to watch for, and what they usually mean

A bad week often looks like: one missed deadline, visible frustration about a specific thing, tiredness that improves after a weekend, still engaging in team banter or offering ideas, even while stressed.

Burnout often looks like: a pattern stretching back weeks or months, flatness rather than frustration, exhaustion that persists regardless of rest, withdrawal from things they used to opt into, a shift from "this is hard" to "what's the point."

If you're not sure which you're looking at, time is often the clearest test. Check in again in two weeks. A bad patch usually has visible peaks and troughs. Burnout tends to be a flat, low line that doesn't move much either way.

---

## What can help

For a bad week, the fix is usually simple: acknowledge it, lighten the load where you can this week specifically, and let it pass. Over-responding to a normal rough patch, extra check-ins, formal conversations, can sometimes make someone feel more watched than supported.

For burnout, the fix has to touch the environment, not just the person:

* Have a direct, private conversation. A genuine "how are you doing, really" with space for a honest answer.
* Look at workload before you look at attitude. If the answer to "why is this happening" is a calendar full of back-to-back meetings and an inbox that never empties, that's your starting point.
* Don't ask them to fix it alone. A person deep in burnout rarely has the capacity to redesign their own workload. That's a shared job between them and their manager.
* Protect any time off they do take. If someone comes back from leave to 400 unread emails and a pile of backlog, the break doesn't actually restore anything.

## Where confidential support fits in

Sometimes what a struggling team member needs isn't a bigger conversation with their manager. It's a confidential space, separate from the org chart, to actually say what's going on without it affecting how they're seen at work. That's the gap 1:1 wellbeing coaching is built to sit in: not a replacement for good management, but a companion to it, for the moments when someone needs to think out loud with someone who isn't their boss.

If you're noticing this pattern across more than one person on your team, that's usually less about any individual and more about the system they're working inside. Worth a conversation.
