Have you ever woken up in the morning and despite sleeping for 7 hours, you feel like you'd been hit by a bus? And you didn't even scroll!

If this sounds familiar, you're not imagining it and you're definitely not alone. Nearly a third of adults in the US sleep less than the seven hours a night that's recommended for basic health, and even among people who technically hit that number, only a little over half say they actually wake up feeling rested.<sup>1</sup> Sleep, it turns out, is not solely about hitting the right amount of hours. Good restful sleep is about quality, timing and what you do with the sixteen hours you're awake for.

I spent years working in clinics watching people run on fumes and brush it off as normal. Then I became one of those people myself. So this one's personal as much as professional.

---

## Sleep debt happens without you even realizing

We tend to picture sleep deprivation as the all-nighter, the red-eye flight or the newborn phase. But most of the tiredness people carry around is the slow accumulation of going to bed forty minutes too late, most nights, for months.

Research on sleep loss consistently shows it chips away at attention, working memory and what's called cognitive flexibility, your ability to shift between tasks and adapt when something unexpected comes up.<sup>2</sup> These cognitive functions need to be sharp if your day involves context-switching between a client call, a school pickup and a busy inbox. You don't need to be sleep-deprived in the extreme sense to feel its effects. You just need to be a little short, over a period of time.

---

## The three places energy leaks

When people come to me exhausted, there are three things that I observe together:

Your body functions best on a predictable schedule. Irregular sleep habits like turning in at 10 p.m. on Tuesday but staying up until 1 a.m. on Friday, confuses your internal clock and disrupts your circadian rhythm.

Bright light first thing in the morning helps set your body's clock for the day. Bright screens right before bed do the opposite as they push that clock later and make it harder to fall asleep even when you're tired.<sup>3</sup> We have to become more intentional about this: morning bright light instead of morning phone, evening dimness instead of evening scrolling.

Treating rest as a reward is a common trap for many people, including myself. When relaxation stays at the bottom of an endless to-do list, it is not going to happen! Rest is the fuel that makes work possible.

---

## So, what can we do?

Let's take small steps in the right direction:

1. Pick one wake time and stick to it, even on weekends. Your wake time influences your whole rhythm more than your bedtime does.
2. Get outside in the first hour of your day, even for ten minutes. Even if it's overcast.
3. Give yourself a solid wind-down, twenty minutes with no screen, before you try to sleep. Read, stretch, sit with a cup of something warm. I love a cup of tea before bed.
4. Notice your natural afternoon dip rather than fighting it with a third coffee. A short walk or five minutes of quiet time can work wonders.
5. Let one thing stay undone at the end of the day. It seems counterintuitive but this practice can help you solidify the notion that rest does not need to be earned.

---

## If you're still exhausted after trying all this

Sometimes the answer may be more than a habit tweak. Persistent exhaustion despite consistent sleep can point to something worth raising with a doctor, from sleep apnea to thyroid issues to burnout that has gone past the point where self-management can resolve it. It's worth ruling out the physical instead of assuming it's just a discipline issue.
