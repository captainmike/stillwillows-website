A wellbeing benefit that nobody uses is, in practice, no benefit at all. The budget is spent and the box is ticked, but the person who is struggling on a Tuesday is still not getting the support.

In our earlier post, [What to Consider When Building a Wellbeing Benefit That Will Fit Your Team](/blog/what-to-consider-when-building-a-wellbeing-benefit-that-will-fit-your-team/), we looked at how to choose something that suits your people. This one picks up where that leaves off: what it takes for people to actually use it.

---

## Where the gap really is

More than 75% of US workers have access to mental health coverage, yet fewer than 45% use it.<sup>1</sup> The people behind that research point to communication as the main gap.

An Integrated Benefits Institute analysis of more than 900 employers and employees found that only about half of employees (49.8%) were aware of their employer's health and wellness programmes. Among those who were aware, 64% used them. The top reasons for not taking part were lack of time (61%), limited interest in what was on offer (37%) and lack of awareness or access (34%).<sup>2</sup>

People skip these benefits because they do not know about them, cannot work out how to use them, or do not have the time. The deeper trust and stigma questions are a different story, and we covered those in [Why Your Team Isn't Using Your EAP](/blog/why-your-team-isnt-using-your-eap/). This post stays with the practical side: what to do once you have chosen a benefit, to give people a real chance to use it.

---

## 1. Make it easy to find and simple to use

In a 2025 NAMI poll, only half of employees knew how to access their benefits.<sup>3</sup> Put everything on one page in plain language: what it is, who it is for, what it costs them (ideally nothing), how to book, and what happens in a first session. If someone needs three logins and a PDF to get started, most will not. As one counsellor interviewed by Canadian HR Reporter put it, make the practical details easy to find.<sup>4</sup>

---

## 2. Put the privacy answer in the announcement

Why privacy doubts run so deep is covered in the EAP post above. For a launch, answer the question before anyone has to ask it. Say it in the first few lines of the announcement, not in the terms and conditions: who sees what, what is never shared with the employer, and the few exceptions, such as a serious risk of harm. In one national poll, 23% of Canadians cited fear of a confidentiality breach as a barrier to using their employee assistance programme.<sup>4</sup> If your employer cannot see who has used the benefit, say so plainly.

---

## 3. Protect the time to use it

Lack of time was the number one reason in the IBI analysis.<sup>2</sup> A benefit that can only be used by squeezing it between meetings, or by explaining yourself to a manager, will stay unused. Say clearly that booking during working hours is fine, that nobody has to say what it is for, and that virtual and flexible options exist. Then show it: when a leader mentions they booked a session or took a proper break, it tells everyone else that it is allowed.

---

## 4. Say it more than once

Lincoln Financial's recommendation is year-round awareness rather than a single push at open enrolment, tied to the moments when pressure peaks, such as review cycles, busy seasons and the return from holiday.<sup>1</sup> Vary the channel as well: a line in the team chat, a reminder in the monthly update, a pinned page. Repeating yourself often feels excessive to the person sending the message and about right to the person receiving it.

---

## 5. Let managers point, not probe

Managers are often the first to notice that someone is struggling. Their role is not to counsel. It is to know the benefit exists, briefly describe it, and leave the decision with the person. If you want help with how that conversation can go without crossing a line, our post on [supporting someone through a hard stretch](/blog/supporting-someone-through-a-hard-stretch/) covers it.

---

## 6. Measure use without watching people

You will want to know whether it is working. Look at overall uptake and anonymous feedback, never individual names. The counsellor quoted by Canadian HR Reporter cautions against treating use of support as a performance measure, and it is easy to see why: the moment people suspect they are being counted, the trust that gets them through the door starts to disappear.<sup>4</sup> A simple quarterly check is enough. How many people used it, what did the team say anonymously, and what should we change?

---

## One honest caveat

Even a well-launched benefit cannot fix an unsustainable workload or a culture people do not trust.<sup>4</sup> If your team is stretched thin, the most effective step may be taking something off their plates, not adding support on top. A benefit works best as one part of a team where it feels safe to use it.
